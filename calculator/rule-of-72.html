History and Concept of the Rule of 72

The Rule of 72 dates back to the 15th century, first documented by the famous Italian mathematician Luca Pacioli (the father of accounting) in his 1494 publication *Summa de arithmetica*. It acts as a mental math shortcut to approximate compound interest doubling cycles without logarithms.

Why the Number 72?

From a pure mathematical standpoint, the natural log of 2 is approximately 0.693 ($69.3\%$). Therefore, a perfect continuous compounding model requires the "Rule of 69.3". However, the number 72 is chosen for mental arithmetic because it is highly divisible by common interest rates (2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 18, 24), making calculation simple without calculators.

Rule of 70 vs. Rule of 72

Depending on interest rates or compound frequencies, some models choose 70 or 69. For daily or continuous compounding, the Rule of 70 or Rule of 69.3 yields slightly more accurate doubling periods. For annual compound loops on typical rates, 72 is standard.

Rule of 72 for Inflation

You can also apply the Rule of 72 to calculate how quickly inflation erodes purchasing power. By dividing 72 by the inflation rate (e.g. 3%), you find that cash purchasing power will halve in approximately 24 years.

Limitations

The Rule of 72 is an approximation. As return rates exceed 15-20%, the mathematical accuracy decreases, as shown in the matrix above. For extreme parameters, actual logarithm calculations are necessary.
