How to Use This Net Worth Calculator

Your net worth is a single number that captures your full financial picture. Track assets (what you own) and liabilities (what you owe) in four steps:

  1. List Your Assets: Add up every dollar you own. Cash, savings, brokerage accounts, retirement accounts, your home's market value, vehicles, and other valuables. Look up current values, not what you originally paid.
  2. List Your Liabilities: Add up every dollar you owe. Mortgage balance, student loans, credit card debt, auto loans, and other debts. Use the current outstanding balance, not the original loan amount.
  3. Calculate Your Net Worth: The calculator does the math: Net Worth = Total Assets − Total Liabilities. A positive number means you own more than you owe. A negative number means debt exceeds assets — a red flag to address.
  4. Review the Health Metrics: The debt-to-asset ratio shows how leveraged you are. A ratio under 40% is healthy. The liquid ratio shows how much of your wealth is in cash/investments vs. illiquid property.

Why Net Worth Matters More Than Income

A high salary doesn't automatically mean wealth. Many six-figure earners have negative net worth because of big mortgages, car loans, and lifestyle inflation. Conversely, someone earning $50,000/yr who lives below their means and invests the difference can have a higher net worth than a $200k earner drowning in debt.

Tracking net worth over time is the single best metric for measuring actual wealth-building. It strips out the noise of income fluctuations, taxes, and one-time events to show whether you're actually getting ahead.

The Net Worth Formula

Net worth is the simplest equation in personal finance, but it requires accurate inputs on both sides:

Net Worth = Total Assets − Total Liabilities
Debt-to-Asset Ratio = (Total Liabilities ÷ Total Assets) × 100
Liquid Ratio = (Liquid Assets ÷ Total Assets) × 100

Asset vs. Liability Variable Guide

Total AssetsCash + savings + investments + retirement + real estate + vehicles + other
Total LiabilitiesMortgage + student loans + credit cards + auto loans + other debts
Liquid AssetsCash + savings + investments (anything you can convert to cash within ~3 days)
Illiquid AssetsReal estate + vehicles + collectibles (harder to convert to cash quickly)
EquityNet worth as a % of total assets — the share of wealth you truly own

Pay Yourself First Tip
The fastest way to grow net worth: automate savings. Before you pay any bills, route 10-20% of your income straight into a retirement or brokerage account. When the money never lands in your checking account, you don't miss it. Over 30 years, this single habit can produce a net worth of $1M+ on a modest income.

Frequently Asked Questions

Net worth is the total value of what you own (assets) minus what you owe (liabilities). It is a single number that summarizes your financial health. Assets include cash, investments, retirement accounts, real estate, and vehicles. Liabilities include mortgages, student loans, credit card balances, and other debts.
A common benchmark is that your net worth should equal your annual salary by age 30, 2x by 40, 4x by 50, and 10x by 60. Median US household net worth is around $192,000 (2022 Federal Reserve data). For comparison, the top 10% of US households have a net worth above $1.2M.
Anything you own that has measurable monetary value: cash, checking and savings accounts, brokerage and retirement accounts (401k, IRA, Roth), real estate (current market value), vehicles (resale value), and personal property worth over a threshold (collectibles, jewelry). Some financial advisors exclude your primary residence from net worth to focus on "investable net worth."
Any money you owe: mortgage balance, student loans, car loans, credit card balances, personal loans, unpaid medical bills, taxes owed, and lines of credit. Include only the outstanding principal balance, not the original loan amount. Future obligations like rent or projected college costs are usually excluded.
In strict accounting terms, your home is an asset because you own it. However, some financial advisors use "investable net worth" which excludes your primary residence because it is not liquid (you'd need to sell it to access the equity). Whether you include it depends on your goal: total net worth vs. retirement-readiness.
Most financial experts recommend tracking net worth monthly or quarterly. Monthly works if you have a simple balance sheet (cash, brokerage, mortgage). Quarterly is better for net worth that includes volatile assets like real estate or business equity. Avoid daily tracking - short-term market swings will mask your real progress.
